# Review of last week

## What went well

1. **You ran the experiment as an experiment.** You stated the independent variable, held the relevant controller and mission settings constant, made a directional prediction, and recorded both outcomes. That is the right autonomy-debugging workflow.

2. **You did not force the data to agree with your eyes.** The stricter gate looked more efficient, but its recorded path was about `0.030 m` longer over roughly `7.15 m`. You correctly questioned visual bias, physics-engine variation, and the weakness of drawing a conclusion from one run per setting.

3. **You improved the analysis where the engineering meaning mattered.** Renaming the accumulated quantity to `actual_planar_distance`, reporting final-goal error, and relabeling the ratio were substantive improvements. Using AI for the bag-reader scaffolding while personally owning the hypothesis, metric, and interpretation is healthy AI leverage.

## Areas to improve

1. **The default parameter was not restored.** The Week 6 commit leaves `heading_tolerance: 0.10` in the repository, although the experiment defined `0.25 rad` as the baseline and the plan required restoring it. Put it back to `0.25 rad` before beginning Week 7. Closing an experiment includes returning the system to its documented baseline.

2. **Scope the conclusion to the metric.** The data does not support the prediction that `0.10 rad` would reduce planar distance. It does not establish that the stricter controller is generally less efficient: the difference is only about `0.4%`, there was one run per condition, and mission duration and curvature were not measured. The best conclusion is **inconclusive, with no observed path-length improvement**.

3. **Cross-track error would not require a spline or controller changes for this mission.** The intended route is already a sequence of straight waypoint-to-waypoint segments. An offline analyzer could compute each odometry point's perpendicular distance to the active line segment. That is a useful future metric, but it is not this week's task.

## What to carry forward

**Separate “my prediction was not observed in this metric” from “the design is worse.” Strong autonomy engineers preserve that distinction when evidence is limited.**

# Week 7 — Characterize IMU measurements against odometry

**Generated:** November 2, 2026

## Weekly goal

Begin the state-estimation phase by determining what Nova Carter's simulated IMU actually reports while stationary and during a controlled yaw rotation.

You will compare:

- IMU angular velocity;
- IMU linear-acceleration magnitude; and
- yaw rate derived from odometry.

The learning loop is:

`predict sensor behavior -> inspect interfaces -> record two short cases -> calculate -> compare -> explain`

This is sensor characterization, not sensor fusion yet. Before trusting an estimator, you need to know the frame, units, sign convention, bias/noise behavior, and whether gravity appears in the measurement.

## Estimated time

**70–90 minutes core**, including restoring the Week 6 baseline, Isaac Sim startup, ROS topic discovery, two short recordings, analysis scaffolding, and troubleshooting.

If finding or configuring the IMU topic consumes substantial time, stop after recording valid stationary and turning bags and documenting the topic, message type, frame, and units. That is legitimate progress.

## Concrete tasks

### 0. Restore the controller baseline — 5 minutes

Change:

```yaml
heading_tolerance: 0.10
```

back to:

```yaml
heading_tolerance: 0.25
```

in `nova_carter/simple_waypoint_node/params/waypoint_node_params.yaml`.

If you launch the waypoint node later, verify the loaded value:

```bash
ros2 param get /waypoint_node heading_tolerance
```

Do not rerun the Week 6 comparison.

### 1. Discover the actual IMU interface and predict its behavior — 10–15 minutes

Start Nova Carter in Isaac Sim, but do not run the mission or waypoint controller. Avoid competing `/cmd_vel` publishers.

Find the IMU topic rather than assuming its name:

```bash
ros2 topic list -t | rg -i 'imu|odom'
ros2 topic info <imu-topic> --verbose
ros2 topic echo <imu-topic> --once
ros2 interface show sensor_msgs/msg/Imu
```

Write down:

- exact topic name;
- `header.frame_id`;
- units for angular velocity and linear acceleration;
- whether orientation is populated;
- what the covariance arrays contain.

Before moving the rover, predict:

1. Stationary angular velocity should be near zero.
2. A commanded positive yaw rotation should produce a consistent sign in IMU `angular_velocity.z` and odometry-derived yaw rate.
3. The stationary acceleration magnitude may be near `9.81 m/s²` or near zero depending on the simulator's IMU convention and configuration. Record your prediction, but treat the observed message as authoritative.

Do not look up the answer after seeing the data and rewrite the prediction.

### 2. Record two short, controlled bags — 15–20 minutes

Use two separate recordings to avoid building event-segmentation infrastructure.

#### Stationary bag

With Nova Carter fully stopped, record approximately 5–10 seconds of:

- the discovered IMU topic;
- `/chassis/odom`; and
- `/cmd_vel`, if it is being published.

Name it something like:

```text
week7_imu_stationary
```

#### Positive-yaw bag

Confirm that only your manual command source will publish `/cmd_vel`:

```bash
ros2 topic info /cmd_vel --verbose
```

Command approximately `+0.3 rad/s` angular velocity with zero linear velocity, then record another 5–10 seconds using the same topics:

```text
week7_imu_positive_yaw
```

Stop the rover and publish a zero command afterward.

Inspect both recordings with `ros2 bag info`. Verify that the IMU and odometry topics are present and that each bag covers the intended behavior.

The exact command-line syntax may depend on your existing control setup. AI assistance for the `ros2 topic pub` syntax is fine; you own the sign, units, and single-publisher safety check.

### 3. Write a small offline comparison — 30–35 minutes

Create a focused script such as:

```text
tools/analysis/analyze_imu_odom.py
```

AI may scaffold:

- opening each rosbag;
- deserializing `sensor_msgs/msg/Imu` and `nav_msgs/msg/Odometry`;
- argument parsing;
- collecting samples by topic; and
- converting the odometry quaternion to yaw using a standard ROS/tf helper.

Personally implement and understand the important calculation:

```python
delta_yaw = atan2(
    sin(current_yaw - previous_yaw),
    cos(current_yaw - previous_yaw),
)
dt = (current_timestamp_ns - previous_timestamp_ns) * 1e-9
odom_yaw_rate = delta_yaw / dt
```

Skip the first sample and any pair with `dt <= 0`. The angle wrapping prevents a crossing between `+pi` and `-pi` from appearing as an enormous rotation.

Also personally calculate:

```python
accel_magnitude = sqrt(ax * ax + ay * ay + az * az)
```

For the stationary bag, print:

- mean IMU `angular_velocity.z`;
- a simple spread measure such as standard deviation or min/max; and
- mean acceleration magnitude.

For the positive-yaw bag, print:

- mean IMU `angular_velocity.z`;
- mean odometry-derived yaw rate; and
- whether their signs agree.

Do not attempt sample-by-sample fusion. The streams may be asynchronous, and comparing summary behavior over a steady interval is sufficient this week.

### 4. Diagnose and explain the measurements — 10–15 minutes

Write 4–6 sentences answering:

- Did the stationary gyro report exactly zero, a small bias/noise value, or something else?
- Did stationary acceleration include gravity?
- What frame and units give those numbers meaning?
- Did positive commanded yaw produce matching signs in IMU and odometry?
- How close were IMU yaw rate and odometry-derived yaw rate, and what could explain a difference?
- Why would integrating even a small gyro bias cause heading error to grow over time?
- Why is odometry a useful reference in this simulation but not automatically ground truth on a physical rover?

If the signs disagree, do not “fix” them immediately. First investigate the IMU frame, odometry frame, axis convention, and transform between them.
